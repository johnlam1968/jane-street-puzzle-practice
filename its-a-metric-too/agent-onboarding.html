How to play this game
  1. The goal. Tile the whole board — every square belongs to a state — and drive every published number to FORCED. Play it in the browser, and treat the browser as your only tool. The Goal and the Rules at the top of the harness page is the whole specification: everything you need in order to succeed is written there, and the page implements it exactly, so do not work out the cost model behind it, do not re-check its costs by hand, and do not write a solver. Careful, reactive playing beats any solver, and it is the only way to end up with a tiling you actually understand.
  2. Stay on the page. The page is the whole world, and it is the whole specification. This note is a folded box and the Rules are one too: click a title to open it — a folded box shows only its title, so if you read one and it looks empty, open it instead of looking elsewhere. Do not go looking for the repository, the page source, the data files, another implementation of this puzzle, or a stored solution kept somewhere else on the machine — and do not download or unpack one either. A tiling you did not play your way to is not a play, and reading the code behind the referee is the same as reading the answer. If something on the page seems to be missing, the answer is on the page: the Rules at the top, the legend at the bottom, the row for that number in the table on the right, or the note under it.
  3. Driving it. A square answers to pointerdown, not to click; the tools and the palette answer to click. A digit 0–9 on the keyboard picks that colour, and that is the reliable way to do it — a palette click can be dispatched and still leave the old colour active, so read aria-pressed back after every control change. There are only ten colours, and two states of the same colour that touch would melt into one state (the page says so when it happens), so give a colour to states that do not touch and reuse it elsewhere. Run the snippet below in the console of the harness page; it does all of it, and nothing else is needed. One stroke is a pointerdown plus a pointerup, and the undo stack grows once per stroke.

  4. Careful. Address a square by its data-i, never by a pixel position: the page scrolls when something is clicked, and a click that lands on the wrong square silently corrupts a state. After every action, read the board back — a click can be dispatched and still leave the old thing active. After switching boards, check the plate name and the published numbers, because the picker and the board can disagree. Every board keeps its own sheet, so painting on one board does not disturb another — and you should not touch a board you were not asked to play.
  5. Mistakes are cheap. You are not expected to get it right the first time: Undo takes back one stroke, and Ctrl+Z (Cmd+Z) does the same, so a wrong shape is one click away from being undone; Clear wipes the sheet and starts over. And you need not keep one attempt: the JSON buttons store the board and read it back. Save JSON downloads it as a file, Copy JSON puts the very same text on the clipboard, Open JSON reads a file back into the page, and Save PNG saves a picture of the board. Save a try under a name, try something else, come back to it. If you drive the page yourself, the same things are reachable without clicking — the ids are in the snippet above: hand the file input a file and it loads at once, and the undo and clear buttons do what their names say. The saved sheet IS that JSON: the states, the assumed capitols and your own constraints, in the shape metric-too-ledger/1.
  6. Every move is written down. The Paint history box lists every action you took, newest first, and every line is a link: click it and the page goes back to the board as it was right after that action — so a wrong shape is one click away, and the browser's own back button walks the same list. The list is JSON too: History JSON writes it to a file, and Open JSON reads such a file back, board and all. So you need not undo one step at a time: jump straight to the state you want to be in, read the board there, and carry on from there. There is a number box as well: type the number of a step and the page goes to that step, or press Enter.
  7. The loop. Paint one state, then read the board before you touch anything else: the badge in the corner of each square, the colour of the walls, the cost in the lower left of each square, the rows of the Verdicts table, and the one-line summary. Each of them tells you which of your assumptions the board just refused, and the colour of that cost says which way to move: black and bold means it agrees with the published number, green means the route you assume is too expensive, amber means it is too cheap. Fix the smallest thing the board complains about, read again, and go on. A complaint is the next instruction, not a reason to stop: do not step out of the game to work out why the board is complaining, and do not stand still to build a theory about it. Do not plan the whole tiling in your head first — the board is a cheap place to try things.
  8. What the board says, and what to do about it.
    • A red wall — the painted region has no symmetry at all, so it is not a state yet: draw it again as a shape with a mirror or a rotation.
    • An amber wall — that state has no capitol, so it goes nowhere and every step inside it costs its whole area: give it a capitol, or draw it again so that it has one.
    • A ✕ badge — a capitol you know is cheaper than the published number, so that number cannot be the minimum: one of your assumptions is wrong. Undo the last move and try the other shape.
    • An ○ badge — the number can be paid, but it is not pinned down yet: size the free squares, or drop an assumption you do not need.
    • An ▲ badge — a capitol that has not been revealed yet has to be nearer: rest the pointer on that square and the squares that could hold it light up with a dashed ring; assume one of them with the capitol tool.
    • A green cost — the route you assume costs more than the published number: make a state on that route smaller.
    • An amber cost — the route you assume costs less than the published number: the real route has to be longer, so something on it must grow, or that capitol is too close.
    • A square that reads 1 — it needs a 1×1 state right next to it, and that state is itself a capitol.
    • A square that reads 0 — that square is the capitol of its own state.
  9. A verdict is what the page answers for one published number. The Verdicts table has one row per published number: the square, the number it reads, the range of costs your painting allows, the verdict, the reason, and the route that proves it. The names mean: FORCED — your assumptions pin the number exactly; leave it alone. TIGHT_CHEAPEST — the cheapest route already costs exactly this, so the number is the minimum. TIGHT_GEODESIC — the known route costs exactly this and nothing may be nearer. NEEDS_EXACT_PATH — the number sits inside the range: you need a route that costs exactly this. NEEDS_HIDDEN_CAPITOL — a capitol that has not been revealed yet has to be strictly nearer. CONTRADICTED — a capitol you know is already cheaper, so one of your assumptions is wrong: undo. UNPRODUCIBLE — no route can pay this number at all; a hidden capitol has to do it. A note is not a verdict — the Notes box under it explains what the page just found, and it usually names the exact square that is wrong.
  10. The texts around the board. The line under the tools — the box right below the undo button — is the summary. Paint a state, or open a board file. means nothing is painted yet. No contradiction: every published number is still reachable under these assumptions. means your assumptions still allow every number. N published numbers are contradicted names the squares that are wrong. The green one, The tiling is complete and every published number is produced and is the cheapest one, means you are there. The chips in the tally count the verdicts. The head of the board says how many states you have, how many of them have a capitol, and how many squares are still unknown. The hint beside the row-sum button says what is still missing: the sheet is not tiled yet, a painted region is not a state yet, an assumed capitol does not match the state it sits in, or N published numbers are not settled yet.
  11. Read the Legends page, which explains every mark on the board. It explains every mark on the board: the area in the corner, the capitol, the two cost numbers, the badge, the wall colours, the ring for a hidden capitol, and your own constraints. Read it once, and read it again whenever a mark means something you did not expect. Some of the marks are colour and not text: when the text is not enough, look at the board — take a screenshot and read the walls, the badges and the rings with your eyes.
  12. When it is done. The one-line summary says the tiling is complete and every published number is settled, and the tally shows nothing left that is open, demanded or contradicted — every number is FORCED. On a practice board the computed answer and the row sums appear under the board; on the 11×11 the page never shows one, because that puzzle is still open. Check your tiling against the published numbers yourself before you believe it.
  13. Reading and saving. The whole sheet sits in localStorage under mt2ledger:<board id>: c is the colour of every square, a the capitols you assumed, con your constraints. Read it instead of scraping the screen. Each board keeps its own sheet, so you can leave and come back to it. The JSON button puts the same sheet on the clipboard as metric-too-ledger/1 — the states, the assumed capitols and the constraints — and the JSON reader takes it back. In the console of the page, colors, assumed and hiddenCapMap can be read directly; the last one maps a square that needs a hidden capitol to the squares that could hold it, so you can skip the wait for the ring.
  14. Never. There is no answer key in this page: the shapes of the solution are not in the page source, on purpose, and if you ever find a page that offers one, that is the answer, not a way to play. Do not write a solver, and do not try to work out the cost model — the page already does it, faithfully, and the point of playing is to let the board tell you. And do not reverse-engineer the hidden-capitol candidates either: hover the number and read the ring, or assume a capitol and let the board judge it. And remember that the page only re-checks the published numbers: it cannot tell you that your tiling is the only one that fits.

That is the whole game. The board is the referee; it never lies about the costs, so use it and do not re-derive anything.
